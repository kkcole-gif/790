Dissertation Prospectus

You write a proposal for your dissertation. It argues there is a problem worth investigating, you are positioned to investigate it, and you have a principled method for doing so. You hand it in twice: a complete draft for the Week 14 workshop, and a revised final version during our exam slot.

What you hand in
A prospectus with four elements: problem, conversation, method, and stakes
Workshop draft
By 11:59 pm, Wednesday, April 14, with a one-paragraph director’s note. Not graded.
Final version
By 11:00 am, Thursday, April 29, our scheduled exam slot
Weight
40% of the final grade
Length
No set length. Write what the four elements need.
Submit
Canvas, email, print, or a Google Drive link

Why you write it

The prospectus is the document your committee approves before you begin the dissertation. Writing a version now, with a cohort to read it, means you meet your committee with a draft which has already survived questions. It also gathers the semester’s work: the identity statement gives you the stakes, the exam list gives you the conversation, and the Week 11 activity gives you the argument for your method.

The four elements

No section structure is required. Organize the document as your argument requires, as long as all four elements are present and substantive.

The problem
Which question you ask and why it matters. Name the phenomenon, tension, or gap with precision. “I am studying social media” names a topic. Problems look like these: “I ask how content moderators decide what counts as public mourning.” “I ask how patient-portal message templates narrow the questions patients bring to clinicians.” “I ask how a mutual aid society composed a public record of itself when the press ignored it, and how its descendants want the record kept now.”
The conversation
Who has taken up the question, and what your project does differently. Make the conversation visible and state your position in it. Your exam list rationale is a first draft of this element.
The method
How you will pursue the question, and why the approach suits the problem. Argue for the method and its methodology together, as in the Week 11 activity.
The stakes
Why the work matters to scholars and to readers beyond the discipline, stated honestly and specifically. Your identity statement is a first draft of this element.

How it develops, step by step

  1. Week 10: seed writing. Bring 300 to 500 words to your individual meeting: “The problem my dissertation addresses is ___, and it matters because ___.”
  2. Week 11 (March 25): first paragraph and method. Bring a first prospectus paragraph, then argue for your method in class.
  3. Week 12 (April 1): stakes. The “so what” gauntlet presses on why the project matters, and a CRDM graduate talks about where a dissertation goes.
  4. Week 13 (April 8): audiences. Three versions of your project for three readers.
  5. By April 14: the workshop draft. Upload a complete draft with a director’s note.
  6. Week 14 (April 15): workshop. Your group responds with the protocol on the Week 14 page.
  7. Week 15 (April 22): presentation. Five to ten minutes on what you are doing, why it matters, and what you are still working out.
  8. By 11:00 am, April 29: the final version.

The workshop draft

Upload a complete draft to Canvas, or share it through Google Drive, by 11:59 pm on April 14. Bring printed copies for your group of three or four if you prefer print. Add a one-paragraph director’s note: what you are least sure of, where you most need feedback, and what feels strong. The workshop is generative. Its purpose is to make the draft better, and the draft is not graded.

A nonacademic prospectus

If your post-degree goals are primarily nonacademic, you have the option of writing a public-facing research proposal or a professional research agenda. It asks for the same rigor, applied to a different problem of audience and form. Talk with me before Week 11. Submit it as a document, an annotated outline, a slide deck with notes, or a combination.

How it is assessed

Complete and substantive
A clear problem, real engagement with scholarship, a justified method, and stakes beyond the field, with independent thinking and momentum. Earns the full 40 points.
Nearly there
One or more elements underdeveloped. Earns 32 points.
Not yet complete
Not submitted, or below the expectations for doctoral work. Come talk to me right away.

Submitting the final version

Submit by 11:00 am on April 29, the close of our scheduled exam slot (8:30 to 11:00 am), through Canvas (PDF or Word), by email to kkcole2@ncsu.edu, in hard copy, or as a Google Drive link. The earlier you raise a concern, the more help I give.

Generative AI

Generative AI is permitted for brainstorming or editing. The problem, the scholarly positioning, the methodological argument, and the stakes must be yours.
