Perusall Annotation Guide

Perusall is where we think before we think together. Annotations are a first draft of your engagement with a text, and the seminar depends on them.

When
Before class begins on Thursday
Where
Perusall, through the Canvas course
Each week
The week page names a specific prompt. The general framework below fills in around it.

In milestone weeks (Weeks 5, 8, 10, and 14), annotate the reading closest to your milestone work in full, and draw on the others in your submission.

A strong annotation thread

Weave four elements together. Labels are unnecessary. The point is thinking on the page.

1. Highlight a passage, and say why (three to six per reading)

Highlight any passage which stops you: it clarifies, troubles, contradicts, or confuses. Then say what caught you. A highlight without a comment does no work.

“Mbembe writes sovereignty is ‘the capacity to define who matters and who does not.’ I keep returning to ‘matters,’ which carries both significance and material consequence. The ambiguity seems deliberate. Is necropolitics about recognition, or about physical survival? Both?”

Too thin: “This is an interesting quote,” or a highlight with no comment.

Confusion counts. “I do not follow what Giroux means by the postmodern divide here. I thought he meant…” is excellent work.

2. A summary in your own words (one per reading)

Somewhere in the thread, distill the central argument in three to five sentences, without quotation, as if for a doctoral colleague in another field.

“Benjamin’s ‘Theses’ argue historicism, history as progress, serves power by suppressing the claims of history’s losers. His alternative is a weak messianism: the past is redeemable if the present recognizes itself as called to answer for what has been destroyed. The hardest claim for me is Thesis IX, where progress is the storm blowing the angel away from the wreckage it watches pile up.”

Too thin: “Benjamin talks about history and how it relates to the present.”

3. Genuine questions (two or three per reading)

Ask questions you cannot answer, the ones you want the room to work on.

“Giroux says education either domesticates or liberates. Does one course do both? I am thinking of a class where students are liberated into a framework they chose no more than the one they left.”

“Brown’s homo oeconomicus assumes economic rationality forms the subject. Where does this leave desire, affect, trauma? Is her silence strategic?”

Too thin: “Is this still relevant today?” or “How does this apply to digital media?”

4. A connection to your research (one per reading)

Connect the reading to your project, your intellectual history, or another course text. Name the mechanism, object, or problem it touches. Disagreement counts: “Giroux’s framework breaks down in online learning because…” is a strong reflection.

“Reading Mbembe beside my work on content moderation, I keep asking who decides what counts as harmful, who holds the sovereign right to make speech invisible. Mbembe gives me language platform governance scholarship keeps circling.”

Reading lead

From Week 2 on, each of you leads one session. Leading means opening the conversation so everyone enters it. Sign up for a slot in Canvas during Week 1.

Before class, on Perusall

  • Annotate more thoroughly than usual. Your annotations model the depth you want from the group.
  • Post a short summary of the reading’s core argument as a thread.
  • Post at least one discussion question you want answered.
  • Respond to at least two classmates’ annotations.

In class

  • Open with a five-minute summary, spoken to the group.
  • Pose your Perusall question as the first prompt.
  • Make room for quieter voices. Return to the text when talk drifts.
  • Close with what remains unresolved and what carries forward.

Moves which help: “Who wants to build on what Sam said?” “Several of us got stuck on this passage in Perusall. Let’s start there.” Moves which stall a room: yes-or-no questions, answering your own question, and letting two or three people carry the hour.

Assessment

Annotations use threshold grading. I read them myself, alongside Perusall’s scoring.

Complete and substantive
Passages chosen for stated reasons, comments which analyze, open questions, a specific connection to your work, and the week’s prompt engaged directly.
Nearly there
The elements are present but stay at the surface: questions answerable by rereading, a generic connection, the prompt touched briefly.
Developing
Highlights without comments, a close paraphrase, or no engagement with the prompt.
Not yet complete
No annotations, or annotations posted after class. Late work counts toward your record but not toward the group’s preparation. If something is getting in the way, tell me before the deadline.

Questions

How long should annotations run?

No word count applies. A substantive thread usually runs 300 to 500 words across its comments. If Perusall flags comments as short, develop them further.

Should I respond to classmates?

Yes, at least once per reading. “I read this differently. I thought Benjamin meant…” is enough.

I understood almost none of the reading. What do I annotate?

Annotate the confusion, specifically. “What does Mbembe mean by the state of exception on page 70? Is this Agamben’s concept, or another?” is a strong annotation. The weakest response to a hard text annotates only the parts you understood.

May I annotate in a language other than English?

Yes. I read Spanish and work with other languages through translation tools. Note the language so classmates join in. Translations are welcome and optional.

How do I reach Perusall?

Through the Canvas course. If you cannot reach a reading, email kkcole2@ncsu.edu before class day.
