Method, Methodology, Epistemology

CRDM researchers work across close reading, rhetorical criticism, digital ethnography, discourse analysis, archival research, network mapping, oral history, participatory action research, and more. The question for your prospectus concerns your problem: which method answers it, and how do you defend the choice on epistemological grounds?

Before class

Write your working research question as specifically as you are able. List three methods which might, in principle, answer it. For each, write one sentence on what the method would let you see or do.

Three levels

TermDefinitionCRDM examples
EpistemologyWhat counts as knowledge, and how we reach it.Is a researcher ever objective? Does meaning reside in texts or in interpretation? Is knowledge situated or universal?
MethodologyThe framework which justifies an approach, given your epistemological commitments.Rhetorical criticism, critical discourse analysis, feminist standpoint theory, interpretivism, critical ethnography, grounded theory.
MethodThe technique for gathering and analyzing material.Close reading, interviews, archival research, platform data collection, participant observation, oral history, corpus analysis.

The most common error in CRDM prospectuses names a method without a methodology. “I will use discourse analysis” is a method claim. “Because language constitutes social reality, discourse analysis suits this question better than content analysis” is a methodological argument.

The sequence

1. The methodology card (5 minutes)

FieldWhat it asksYour answer
Research questionStated as a question. 
MethodThe technique or techniques you plan to use. 
MethodologyThe framework grounding the method. What does the method assume about knowing? 
The argumentIn one sentence, why your question requires this method. 
Ruled-out alternativeAnother method which might address the question, and why your methodology rules it out. 
Greatest uncertaintyWhere the argument feels weakest. 

2. Partner pressure test (10 minutes)

Trade cards. Ask your partner three questions and take notes on their card.

  1. Would a different method answer this question as well? What would rule it out?
  2. Does the epistemological claim do work here, or would another framework leave everything unchanged?
  3. What is the strongest counterargument to this method choice, and how would you answer it?

3. Discovery round (10 minutes)

Each of you names one mismatch, gap, or assumption the pressure test exposed. One observation each, without extended explanation. The most common mismatch pairs a critical methodology with a positivist method, or the reverse.

4. Draft the paragraph (15 minutes)

Write three to five sentences which state your research question, name your method and the methodology grounding it, and argue for your approach against a named alternative. The paragraph belongs in your prospectus.

5. Volunteers read (5 minutes)

Two or three of you read aloud. For each, the group answers one question: is the epistemological argument visible?

Tensions particular to CRDM

  • Interpretive and critical approaches. Interpretive research seeks meaning from within a situation. Critical research exposes and contests power. The two are distinct epistemological projects, and methods serve them unevenly.
  • Humanistic and social-scientific modes. In close reading, rhetorical criticism, and archival work, the researcher’s judgment helps constitute the knowledge produced. Surveys, content analysis, and experiments make different claims about validity and generalizability. Hybrid projects work when they name the tension.
  • Digital methods. Network analysis, corpus analysis, and platform data collection often carry positivist assumptions about what data reveals. An interpretive or critical methodology has to interrogate them.

After class

  • Revise the paragraph and add it to your prospectus draft before Week 14.
  • If your framework still feels unsettled, talk with your chair before writing the full methodology section.

Further reading

  • Norman Denzin and Yvonna Lincoln, eds., Handbook of Qualitative Research, the introduction on paradigms.
  • Carolyn Miller, “The Presumptions of Expertise,” on epistemology and ethos in technical communication.
  • danah boyd and Kate Crawford, “Critical Questions for Big Data.”
  • Safiya Umoja Noble, Algorithms of Oppression.
