Activity handout · Week 13 · 45 minutes

How Knowledge Travels

Which venues matter, what each genre does, how long publication takes, and what an abstract is for: students with closely engaged advisors learn these things early, and others finish the degree without them. This activity puts the knowledge on the table, and it bears on the prospectus, because where your work will go shapes how you frame it now.

Before class

Bring one piece of scholarly communication from your field you admire: a journal article, a book chapter, or a public essay. Know its genre, its venue, and what its opening paragraph does. Be ready to say why you chose it.

The sequence

1. Venue mapping (20 minutes, full group)

Each of you contributes:

  • Two or three journals where your work would fit. Skip the general prestige titles in favor of the ones where your work would land.
  • One conference where your work would be welcome.
  • One public venue for a non-specialist version.

Then discuss: who built the prestige hierarchy in your subfield? Does it reflect your work or field convention? Which venues welcome interdisciplinary CRDM work across communication studies and adjacent fields?

2. Two abstracts (20 minutes, alone and then with a partner)

  1. A 250-word conference abstract: the problem, its place in the scholarship, the method, the argument, and why it matters to the field.
  2. A 100-word public pitch: no jargon, opening with the human problem, saying why a non-specialist should care.

Trade both with a partner. What changes between the versions, and what stays? Which gives the more honest account of why the work matters?

3. Close (5 minutes)

Name the first venue you are aiming for. Is your prospectus framed for it, or for another?

Genre reference

A working map, short of a complete taxonomy.

GenreLengthTimelineWhat it does
Conference paper6 to 20 pages, or 15 to 20 minutesSubmit 6 to 12 months aheadStakes a claim in an ongoing conversation and gathers feedback before publication. Often becomes an article.
Journal article6,000 to 12,000 words6 to 24 months or more, with peer reviewThe primary unit of scholarly currency in most CRDM subfields. Establishes a contribution to a specific conversation.
Book chapter5,000 to 10,000 words12 to 36 monthsA contribution to an edited collection, often invited, and useful for interdisciplinary audiences.
Scholarly monograph70,000 to 100,000 words or more3 to 8 years from the dissertationA sustained argument across a field, and the primary currency for humanities tenure.
Working paper or preprintVariableImmediateCirculates work before peer review for faster feedback. Growing in the humanities.
Review essay2,000 to 5,000 words3 to 6 monthsSynthesizes and evaluates recent work, and positions you in a conversation. Often invited.
Public essay or op-ed800 to 3,500 wordsDays to weeksCarries scholarly argument to a general audience and builds public presence. Asks for different skills.
Policy brief2 to 6 pagesVariableDistills research for decision-makers. Increasingly expected for grant-funded work.

What a strong abstract does

An abstract persuades a busy reader to keep reading. Abstracts fail most often for five reasons:

  • They describe the project (“this paper will examine”) in place of arguing it (“I argue”).
  • They bury the intervention in the last sentence.
  • They lean on undefined jargon, which persuades only reviewers who already agree.
  • They never state why the argument matters to the field.
  • They omit the method or archive, so reviewers have no way to judge the contribution.

A strong abstract makes four moves in 250 words: it opens with a problem or provocation, states the intervention within three sentences, grounds it in method or archive, and closes with significance.
