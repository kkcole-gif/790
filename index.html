About the course

CRDM 790 is the last course you take as a coursework student in the Ph.D. program in Communication, Rhetoric, and Digital Media. The program joins rhetorical and communication theory, the study of visual, oral, written, and digital media, and the critical analysis of networked societies. The seminar asks you to draw this range together, from the required courses and electives of earlier semesters, into a line of inquiry you will carry into the dissertation.

The course does three things at once. It gives the cohort a shared critical vocabulary, a set of texts we read, argue about, and carry into individual projects. It scaffolds the practical work of candidacy: your committee, your comprehensive exam list, and your prospectus. It also asks what kind of scholar you are becoming, what you want your work to do, and what comes after the degree, in whatever form it takes. The three movements of the schedule follow this order.

Course goals

By the end of the term, you will be able to:

  1. Articulate a scholarly identity: who you are as a researcher, which questions you pursue, and why they matter.
  2. Read foundational texts in critical theory, political philosophy, and humanistic inquiry, and put them to work across CRDM specializations.
  3. Assemble a dissertation committee suited to your project, and begin the relational work of doctoral mentorship.
  4. Construct a comprehensive exam list which reflects your specialization, your committee’s expertise, and a defensible rationale.
  5. Draft a dissertation prospectus, or an equivalent research proposal, which states a problem, places it in existing scholarship, proposes a method, and outlines the chapters.
  6. Argue for the fit among your research question, method, and epistemology.
  7. Map the genres of academic and public knowledge, identify venues for your work, and plan how your research will circulate.
  8. Practice the generosity which sustains a scholarly community: workshopping, building on, and challenging one another’s work.

Schedule

Each week page lists what to read and bring, how the session runs, and what follows. Current students, alumni, and program faculty join us across the term.

Movement I · Weeks 1 to 5

A shared critical vocabulary, and a first account of who each of you is as a scholar. Readings from Giroux, Walter Benjamin, Mbembe, Ruha Benjamin, and Brown.

  • Week 2Jan 21Critical Pedagogy and the Scholar-CriticGiroux on critical pedagogy, 1979 and 2004. Institutional autopsy.
  • Week 3Jan 28The Past Is Not PastGuest. Benjamin, Illuminations. Wreckage and promise gallery. A current doctoral student joins us.
  • Week 4Feb 4Who Gets to LiveMbembe and Ruha Benjamin. Concept translation workshop.
  • Week 5Feb 11The University Under NeoliberalismDue: Identity Statement. Brown on neoliberalism and the university. Identity statement exchange.

Movement II · Weeks 6 to 10

What Do I Know and Who Helps Me Know It?

Committees, exam lists, and the labor behind both. Readings from Federici, Delgado Bernal and Villalpando, Fisher, Haraway, and Chakravartty and colleagues.

  • Week 6Feb 18The Labor of Knowledge and the Labor of CommitteesGuest. Federici, Caliban and the Witch. A recently advanced student joins us. Committee email workshop.
  • Week 7Feb 25Hauntology: What Haunts Your Field?Fisher on hauntology. Rapid presentations. First-pass exam list, tagged by field.
  • Week 8Mar 4Exam List WorkshopDue: Committee Confirmation. Structured peer review of working draft lists.
  • Week 9Mar 11Situated Knowledges: Revising the ListGuest. Haraway and “#CommunicationSoWhite.” A program faculty member joins us. Canon audit of the draft lists.
  • Week 10Mar 11–24Individual MeetingsDue: Draft Exam List. No group session. Individual meetings and prospectus seed writing.
  • Spring break, March 15 to 19. No session.

Movement III · Weeks 11 to 15

The prospectus, and the question of what the work is for. Readings from Smagorinsky and Ghosh, and three model prospectuses.

  • Week 11Mar 25Method and the Dissertation ImaginationSmagorinsky on the method section. Model prospectus sketches. Method, methodology, epistemology.
  • Week 12Apr 1What Can Humanistic Imagination Actually Do?Guest. Ghosh, The Great Derangement. The “so what” gauntlet. A CRDM graduate joins us.
  • Week 13Apr 8Paths Forward: Alt-Ac and OtherwiseGuest. Graduates working beyond the academy join us. Three versions of one project.
  • Week 14Apr 15Prospectus WorkshopDue: Prospectus draft. Small-group workshop on full drafts.

Milestones

The course uses threshold grading. Each milestone is assessed as complete and substantive (full points), nearly there (80 percent), or not yet complete (no points). A nearly-there milestone revised to the complete threshold earns its full points. Submit through Canvas, by email, in hard copy, or as a Google Drive link.

  • Scholarly Identity Statement, due Week 5 (Feb 11), before class. 20% of the final grade. About 500 words on who you are as a scholar and what your research is for.
  • Committee Confirmation, due Week 8 (Mar 4), before class. 15% of the final grade. Your chair's confirmation, or a plan naming faculty, the status of each conversation, and a timeline. A credible plan meets the threshold.
  • Draft Exam List, due by March 24, the close of the Week 10 meeting window. 25% of the final grade. The list, revised after peer review, the canon audit, and your committee's responses, with a rationale naming the fields it answers to and your position in each.
  • Dissertation Prospectus, due in two stages: a workshop draft before Week 14 (Apr 15), and the final version by 11:00 am on Apr 29, the close of our scheduled exam slot. 40% of the final grade. A problem, its place in the scholarship, a method, stakes beyond the field, and a chapter outline. Nonacademic equivalents are welcome.

Support through exams

Exam preparation is the most solitary stretch of the degree. The course builds two structures to last beyond April. In Week 10, you draw a support constellation, a companion to your Week 1 map, which names the people, places, and practices sustaining your work, and you pair with a writing partner. From Week 11 through April 22, the writing commons meets for an optional hour after class on Thursdays, and partners trade one page a week. Both continue as long as you and your partner find them useful.

Access and comfort

Every session page and handout comes in more than one format. If a format creates a barrier for you, email me, and you will have an alternative before the next session. If you hold an accommodation letter, share it with me in the first two weeks.

Sessions run close to three hours. Bring food. Leave the room whenever you need to, without asking. You are welcome here as a whole person.
