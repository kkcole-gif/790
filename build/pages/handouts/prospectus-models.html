Three Model Prospectus Sketches

A full prospectus runs many pages. These sketches compress three to one page each, so you see the five elements the program requires and how each project fills them. They are illustrations written for this course, drawn from the same three projects as the examples in the milestone guides. Real prospectuses from CRDM graduates will join them as they become available.

Read one sketch closely before Week 11. In class, we take it apart: where does each element begin, what does it claim, and what would a committee member ask first?

1. Platform and media studies, faculty career

Working title: Sorted Out of View: Content Moderation and the Politics of Public Mourning

The problem
After a disaster, platforms decide which posts of grief stay visible. I ask how moderation systems, human and automated, sort public mourning, and whose losses fall out of view.
The conversation
Platform studies has explained moderation as labor and as policy. Rhetorical work on public memory has studied mourning as address to a community. I join the two, because neither accounts for mourning a system removes before any community sees it.
The method
A comparative case study of three disasters, using platform policy documents, archived posts, and interviews with eight to twelve moderators. The interviews need IRB review, which I plan to submit in the summer after my exams.
The stakes
Recovery aid often follows visibility. If moderation shapes whose grief is seen, it shapes who gets help, and the people making those decisions rarely know it.
The chapter outline
One: moderation as a rhetoric of visibility. Two: policy documents and the category of “graphic content.” Three: the three disasters, compared. Four: moderators’ accounts of their own judgments. Five: design and policy recommendations.

2. Technical and professional communication, industry research

Working title: Asking Within the Lines: Patient Portals and the Genre of the Question

The problem
Patient portals give patients structured forms for writing to their care teams. I ask how those forms narrow the questions patients bring, and which questions go unasked.
The conversation
Health communication measures portal use and satisfaction. Rhetorical genre theory explains how forms shape what people are able to say. Usability research tests interfaces for efficiency. I bring genre theory to the portal, because the other two fields treat the form as neutral.
The method
A genre analysis of message templates from three health systems, paired with think-aloud sessions in which twenty patients write a message about a real concern. The sessions need IRB review and a partner site agreement.
The stakes
A question a patient never sends is a symptom no one hears. The findings go to portal designers as much as to scholars, and I plan an industry white paper alongside the dissertation.
The chapter outline
One: the portal message as genre. Two: what three health systems’ templates allow. Three: patients writing, aloud. Four: the unasked question. Five: redesign principles, written for practitioners.

3. Community-engaged and archival rhetoric, public humanities

Working title: Keeping Our Own Record: A Mutual Aid Society and the Rhetoric of Self-Documentation

The problem
A Black mutual aid society in eastern North Carolina kept minutes, ledgers, and programs for sixty years while the local press ignored it. I ask how the society composed a public record of itself, and how its descendants want the record kept now.
The conversation
Black feminist theory of the archive asks what records leave out. Histories of Black mutual aid recover institutions. Community-engaged archival work asks who owns the result. History claims the project first, and rhetoric supplies the question of how the society wrote itself into being.
The method
Rhetorical analysis of the society’s documents, oral history interviews with descendants, and a co-designed digital archive governed by a community agreement. The interviews need IRB review. The agreement comes first, so the community sets the terms before I collect anything.
The stakes
The town’s official history still leaves the society out. The archive returns the record to the people it belongs to, and the dissertation shows how a community argued for its own existence on paper.
The chapter outline
One: self-documentation as rhetoric. Two: the minutes as public address. Three: the ledgers and the ethics of care. Four: descendants on what the record should do. Five: building the archive together, and what the academy owes in return.

Questions for any prospectus

  • Do you find each of the five elements in one or two sentences?
  • Does the method answer the question the problem poses, or a nearby one?
  • Which field claims the project first, and does the conversation element say so?
  • Would a reader outside the field say why it matters?
  • Does the chapter outline show a project someone would finish in two years?
