What the list prepares you for

Under the CRDM academic policies, the preliminary exam has a written part and an oral part. The written part asks you to answer questions from your committee, each in up to four hours, with all answers completed within 72 hours. The oral exam runs about two hours and often includes your prospectus. Your committee must vote unanimously to pass you. Read your list with those questions in mind: which texts will you need to argue with, under time, and possibly without notes?

Why you write it

Your comprehensive exam tests whether you know the fields your dissertation answers to. The list defines those fields for you and your committee. It also makes an argument: it says which conversations your project joins, which it sets aside, and where you stand. A list with no argument is a bibliography. A list with one tells your committee what kind of scholar you are becoming. The draft need not be final, but it must be real.

What you hand in

Part 1: the list

A working list with full citations, organized by the logic your project calls for: theme, method, genealogy, period, or a combination. Give each section a heading which says what it does for the project. Mark any section still to be determined.

The program sets no number or length for lists. Your committee does, so ask your chair early. At this stage the structure matters more than the count. The folder of past CRDM exam lists shows how earlier students organized theirs. Read several, and notice which structures argue and which only sort.

Part 2: the rationale

One substantive paragraph stating the argument of the list: why these texts, in this configuration, prepare you for this project. The rationale also names the fields your dissertation will answer to and the position from which you enter each one.

  • If your project sits within one discipline, locate your position in it. Which conversation does the list join, and which does it set aside?
  • If your project is interdisciplinary, map where the fields meet your problem, and state which field claims the project first.

Falls short: “These texts are foundational to my field and will prepare me for the exam.”

These example rationales are illustrations written for this guide, drawn from three kinds of CRDM projects. Each label names the field, the goal after the degree, and where the questions came from.

Platform and media studies · faculty career

Closer: “My list is organized around three intersecting questions: how digital platforms mediate political subjectivity, how critical geography and STS have theorized surveillance, and what feminist media scholars have argued about algorithmic visibility. I include thinkers from adjacent fields rarely assigned in CRDM courses, because my project needs their vocabulary.”

Technical and professional communication · industry research

Closer: “My list moves from rhetorical genre theory to usability research to health communication, because my project asks how an interface genre teaches patients what counts as a question. Health communication claims the project first. Genre theory gives me a vocabulary the health literature lacks. I set aside most of the clinical research on patient satisfaction, which measures outcomes my project treats as symptoms.”

Community-engaged and archival rhetoric · public humanities

Closer: “My list has three parts: Black feminist theory of the archive, histories of Black mutual aid in the South, and scholarship on community-engaged digital archives. History claims the project first, since my evidence is historical, and rhetoric supplies the questions about how the society composed its own record. The third section answers to my community partners as much as to my committee.”

How the list develops, step by step

  1. Week 7 (February 25): ten texts. Bring ten texts you expect on your list, each tagged with its field and one sentence on why it belongs. Browse the past lists first.
  2. Week 8 (March 4): a working draft for peer review. Bring the list in sections with a draft rationale, plus one past list you admire or would argue with. A partner responds in writing to the five prompts below.
  3. Week 9 (March 11): the canon audit. With Haraway in hand, you audit your own draft and two past lists, then make one addition and one cut.
  4. March 11 to 24: revise with your committee. Fold in what your chair and committee members say, and bring the list to your Week 10 meeting.
  5. By March 24: submit the graded version.

Peer review in Week 8

Your reviewer responds in writing to five prompts:

  1. The organizing logic I see.
  2. A gap I notice: a tradition, period, perspective, or demographic absent from the list.
  3. A text I would add, and why.
  4. The most interesting tension on the list.
  5. One question for the author about the choices behind it.

Bring a working draft, even a rough one. The workshop fails without one.

How it is assessed

Complete and substantive
A defensible organizing logic, a rationale which argues, and awareness of what the list includes, excludes, and why. Earns the full 25 points.
Nearly there
A list with a descriptive rationale, an unclear logic, or no acknowledgment of gaps. Earns 20 points, and a revision to the complete threshold earns the full 25.
Not yet complete
Not submitted by March 24, or submitted without a rationale. Come talk to me.

Generative AI

Generative AI is permitted for formatting citations or checking bibliographic details. The selection of texts and the rationale must be yours, because the list is an argument about your project, and your committee will examine you on it.
