Scholarly Identity Statement

You write about 500 words, in your own voice, on who you are as a scholar: the questions which drive you, where they came from, and why they matter. You hand it in before class in Week 5, and we read the statements together in class.

Why you write it

Every later milestone asks you to choose: which faculty to ask, which texts to read, which problem to pursue. Those choices come easier once you have said, plainly, what you care about and why. The statement gives you this account early, while it still shapes the decisions. You will return to it when you write the rationale for your exam list and the stakes section of your prospectus.

The prompt

Who are you as a scholar? What do you care about, what animates your research, and why does it matter? Where did your intellectual questions come from, and what do you want your work to do in the world?

How to write it, step by step

  1. Week 3 or 4: freewrite for twenty minutes. Answer the prompt without stopping to edit. Use the entry points below if you stall.
  2. Find your sentence. Read the freewrite and underline the one sentence closest to what you believe. Build the statement around it.
  3. Draft in four moves. Name the questions you pursue. Say where they came from, in your reading, your work, or your life. Say why they matter, inside your field and beyond it. Name the people your work speaks with and speaks to.
  4. Read it aloud. Cut any sentence a stranger in your field would have written. Keep the ones only you would write.
  5. Check it against the table below, then submit before class on February 11.

Some ways in. Use the ones which help, and skip the rest.

  • What brought you to your research questions? What is the origin story?
  • What question keeps you up at night?
  • What would be lost if your research did not exist?
  • With whom is your work in conversation, and whom is it for?
  • If a skeptical colleague asked why it matters, what would you say?

What the statement is, and what it is not

A strong statement names things. It reads as the work of a person who cares about something, and it explains why the work matters beyond the field. Four kinds of writing fall short: a summary of your CV or application materials, a list of topics, a statement written for a search committee, and a description of what you plan to study.

Falls short: “My research interests include digital rhetoric, social media, and public discourse. I have presented at several conferences and plan to study online communities.”

Closer: “I started asking how platforms decide whose grief counts after watching my town’s flood become a trending topic for a weekend and then vanish. I study how moderation systems sort public mourning, because the people sorted out of view are the ones the recovery money also misses.”

Read the statement aloud before you submit it, then ask the questions below.

How it is assessed

Complete and substantive
States intellectual purpose and why the research matters, with conviction and specificity. Earns the full 20 points.
Nearly there
Engages the prompt but reads as written for a committee, leaves the stakes unclear, or reads as a topic list. Earns 16 points, and a revision to the complete threshold earns the full 20.
Not yet complete
Not submitted, or below the expectations for doctoral reflection. Come talk to me.

Submitting

Upload a PDF, Word file, or plain text to Canvas, email it to kkcole2@ncsu.edu, or bring a print copy to class. Every format counts the same. If a format creates a barrier, email me.

Generative AI

Generative AI is permitted for brainstorming or light editing. The substance of the statement must be yours, in your voice, from your own experience and convictions. The reason is practical. The statement exists to surface what you think and want, and no model has access to either. If you are unsure whether a use fits, ask me before you submit.
