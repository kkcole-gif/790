Marginalia

We read with pens, in our own copies, and bring the marked pages to the table. Writing in the margin is one of the oldest scholarly practices we have. This guide places your reading in its company and offers a practice to carry from this seminar into your exam list.

Why the margin

In a few months, each of you will read 50 to 80 texts alone, against a list, and need your notes long after the reading. The comprehensive exam rewards a reader who has already argued with a text on its own pages. Marginalia train such a reader. They also stay with you: the copy you mark this spring is the copy you return to for the list, the prospectus, and the dissertation.

A tradition in six scenes

1. The gloss

Medieval scholars read the Bible through the Glossa Ordinaria, a body of commentary compiled in the twelfth century and set between the lines and down the margins of the scriptural text. The Talmud page goes further. Its printed layout places the Mishnah and Gemara at the center, with Rashi’s commentary on one side and the Tosafot on the other, so a single page holds an argument across centuries. Both make the same claim about reading: a text arrives surrounded by its readers, and to read is to join them.

2. The margin as image

In Gothic manuscripts, the margins fill with monkeys, hybrids, knights fighting snails, and figures who mock the sacred text beside them. Michael Camille’s Image on the Edge reads these drawings as a second voice on the page, playful, irreverent, sometimes subversive, contesting what the center claims. The margin, for Camille, is a place where authority gets talked back to.

3. Reading for use

Renaissance readers marked books in order to act on them. Lisa Jardine and Anthony Grafton’s study of Gabriel Harvey shows him reading Livy with pen in hand, preparing counsel for the patrons he served. William Sherman’s Used Books follows the period’s ordinary marks, among them the pointing hand, or manicule, drawn beside a passage worth finding again. Readers also wrote headings and indexes into their copies. These readers built finding aids for their future selves. So will you.

4. The commonplace book

From Erasmus forward, students copied passages into notebooks under headings of their own making, and John Locke published a method for indexing them. A commonplace book turns scattered reading into an organized store of quotation and argument. Your reading notebook for this course belongs to this tradition, and it will feed your exam list directly.

5. Writers in the margin

Some of the sharpest criticism in English first appeared in pencil. William Blake filled his copy of Joshua Reynolds’s Discourses with furious disagreement. Samuel Taylor Coleridge annotated widely, often in books borrowed from friends, and he brought the Latin word marginalia into English use. Charles Lamb, reading Coleridge’s notes in his copy of Samuel Daniel, wrote to him, “I wish every book I have were so noted.” Edgar Allan Poe turned the form into a published genre with his “Marginalia” columns. Herman Melville’s markings in Shakespeare and Milton, now collected at Melville’s Marginalia Online, let us watch Moby-Dick take shape in someone else’s pages. And Pierre de Fermat’s note in his copy of Diophantus, claiming a proof the margin was too narrow to hold, set mathematicians a problem for three and a half centuries.

6. Annotation now

Remi Kalir and Antero Garcia name five things annotation does: it provides information, shares commentary, sparks conversation, expresses power, and aids learning. Christina Sharpe, whom we read in Week 4’s further reading, writes of Black annotation and Black redaction as practices for reading archives built to erase. Both raise a question about our own tools. When annotation moves onto a platform, the platform holds the notes and often scores them. We keep ours on paper, or in files we own, and we share them in the room.

Your practice

Mark with a key

Settle on a small set of marks and use them every week. A starting key:

  • A vertical line beside a central claim.
  • A box around a term the author defines or depends on.
  • An arrow at a move: where the argument turns, concedes, or escalates.
  • A star beside a source to chase. Several will end up on your exam list.
  • A question mark where you disagree or do not follow.
  • Your own sign for a link to your project. Harvey would recognize a manicule.

Write sentences in the margin

Marks find a passage again. Sentences record what you thought of it. Write at least a few: a question to the author, an objection, a connection to another reading, a translation of a dense claim into your own words. Confusion counts, so mark it and say where it starts.

Build an index in the back pages

On the last blank page, list page numbers under your own headings: terms, claims you will cite, passages for class, material for your project. Ten minutes at the end of a reading saves hours in October of next year.

Keep a reading notebook

After each reading, give it one page: the citation, the argument in three sentences, two passages copied out, the key terms, your quarrel with it, and where it might sit on your exam list. Keep the notebook in whatever form you will still have in two years.

Choose your medium

Paper, a PDF annotator, Zotero, typed notes, or voice memos all work, as long as the notes stay yours and you find them again. If a reading’s format gets in the way of annotating it, email me, and I will send another format.

Each week

  • Bring your annotated copy, in print or on a screen.
  • Mark two passages to read aloud, and know why you chose them.
  • Bring one question you want the room to work on.
  • The week page names a specific focus. Give it your sharpest attention.

Reading lead

From Week 2 on, each of you leads one session. Sign up in Canvas during Week 1.

  • By 5 pm the day before class, post a reading note to the week’s Canvas discussion: the core argument in a paragraph, two passages with your marginal comments, and one question to open the session.
  • In class, open with a five-minute account of the reading and pose your question.
  • Make room for quieter voices, and return to the page when talk drifts.
  • Close with what remains unresolved and what carries forward.
