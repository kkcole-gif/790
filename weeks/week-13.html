Movement III · Week 13 · Thursday, April 8

Paths Forward: Alt-Ac and Otherwise

Graduates working beyond the academy join us to talk about what the PhD prepares you for. This week asks how to describe your expertise to different readers, and how to design a prospectus which serves more than one future.

Read
No required reading
Bring
Three 100-word versions of your project, and one piece of scholarly writing you admire
Guests
CRDM graduates working outside the academy
Due
No milestone this week

Before class

Write and bring

  • Three versions of the same project description, 100 words each: for a hiring committee in your field, for a foundation or nonprofit grant panel, and for a journalist writing about your research area.
  • One piece of scholarly communication from your field you admire: an article, a chapter, or a public essay. Know its genre, its venue, and what its opening paragraph does.

Questions to carry into the reading

  • What has the PhD given you beyond your content area: which skills, frameworks, and habits of thought?
  • How do you describe your expertise to someone in a hiring context outside your field?
  • What would a public version of your project look like: a policy brief, an essay, a community partnership?
  • What do you fear losing if you leave, or never enter, the tenure track? What might you gain?
  • How do you design a dissertation which opens doors?

In class

I went from my undergraduate degree into a PhD and then into a tenure-track position, a path few now follow. Our guests know this terrain better than I do. We ask them for candor over polish.

How knowledge travels

Which venues matter, what each genre does, and what an abstract is for are usually learned through mentorship and chance. This activity makes the knowledge explicit.

  1. Venue mapping, as a full group: name two or three journals where your work would fit, one conference where it would be welcome, and one public venue for a non-specialist version.
  2. Discuss who built the prestige hierarchy in your subfield, and which venues welcome interdisciplinary CRDM work.
  3. Draft two abstracts: a 250-word conference abstract (problem, scholarship, method, argument, stakes) and a 100-word public pitch opening with the human problem.
  4. Trade both with a partner. What changes between the versions? Which gives the more honest account of why the work matters?
  5. Close: name the first venue you are aiming for, and ask whether your prospectus is framed for it.

After class

  • Finish your full prospectus draft, due before Week 14, with a one-paragraph director’s note.
  • If your post-degree goals are primarily nonacademic and you have not yet talked with me about an alternative prospectus, do so this week.

Further reading

  • Karen Kelsky, The Professor Is In, the chapters on careers beyond the academy.
  • Kathleen Fitzpatrick, Generous Thinking, on public scholarship and work with communities beyond the academy.
