Movement II · Week 6 · Thursday, February 18

The Labor of Knowledge and the Labor of Committees

Federici asks whose labor built the fields you inherit. The second half of the session turns her question toward your own degree: whose labor will it require, and on what terms do you ask for it?

Read
Federici, Caliban and the Witch, Introduction and Chapter 2 (about 71 pages)
Bring
A committee map and a draft email to your first committee contact
Guest
A recently advanced CRDM student
Due
No milestone this week

Before class

Read

  • Silvia Federici, Caliban and the Witch: Women, the Body, and Primitive Accumulation (Autonomedia, 2004). Introduction, pages 7 to 20, and Chapter 2, “The Accumulation of Labor and the Degradation of Women,” pages 75 to 131. On Perusall.
  • Read slowly. The argument is dense, and pages 91 to 109, on the enclosure of women’s bodies and reproductive labor, reward a second pass.

Write and bring

  • A Perusall annotation on one passage in Federici bearing on your own field, connecting her argument to a dynamic you recognize.
  • A committee map. Name each position (chair, readers, outside member), write two or three sentences on why each person, and note whom you have already spoken with.
  • A draft of the email you will send your first committee contact. We workshop it in class.

Questions to carry into the reading

  • Federici reads the witch hunts as a political campaign to discipline women’s bodies at the dawn of capitalism. What does her argument require you to revise about early modernity?
  • Primitive accumulation usually refers to land and labor. Federici extends the concept to bodies and knowledge. Where does the extension appear in the fields you inherit?
  • Whose labor built your field? Whose names appear in its canon, and whose do not?
  • What has your field enclosed or suppressed? Which knowledges has it made to look like non-knowledge?
  • How does Federici’s materialism sit with the discursive frameworks of Mbembe and Brown?
  • Committee service is labor, much of it uncounted. What do you owe the faculty you ask to serve, and what do you expect from them in return?

Committee email workshop

The workshop turns your committee map into a first message. A strong first ask runs about three sentences: what the project is, why this person, and what you are asking for.

  1. Trade draft emails with a partner.
  2. Respond to your partner’s email in writing for ten minutes. Is the ask clear? Does the email convey the project’s stakes? Is it warm without being overfamiliar?
  3. Discuss your responses, five minutes per email.
  4. As a group, name the common problems: an ask too vague to answer, too much detail for a first contact, a request for commitment before interest.
  5. Revise. Then name the person you will write to first and the day you will send the email.

After class

  • Send at least one committee email within 48 hours of class.
  • Keep any replies. They inform your exam list in Weeks 8 and 9.
  • If you are unsure whom to ask, or how, email me before Week 8.

Further reading

  • Saidiya Hartman, “Venus in Two Acts,” on the archive, its silences, and humanistic imagination. It pairs with next week.
  • María Lugones, “Heterosexualism and the Colonial/Modern Gender System,” a decolonial feminist extension of Federici.
  • Karen Kelsky, The Professor Is In, the chapters on choosing advisors and committee dynamics. Library Reserve.
