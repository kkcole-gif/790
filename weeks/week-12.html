What Can Humanistic Imagination Actually Do?

Ghosh asks why literary imagination has failed to reckon with climate change. This week asks what your dissertation is for, and a CRDM graduate joins us to talk about where a dissertation goes after the degree.

Before we meet

Read

  • Amitav Ghosh, The Great Derangement: Climate Change and the Unthinkable (University of Chicago Press, 2016). Part 1, “Stories,” pages 3 to 84. PDF in the course Readings folder. Open it signed in with your NC State account.
  • Part 1 carries the central argument. Parts 2 and 3, on history and politics, extend it for anyone who wants more.

Read with a pen. The marginalia guide describes the practice.

Write and bring

  • An audience test: a 150-word version of your project for a curious, educated reader outside your field. What do you keep? What falls away?
  • Your marked copy, with a note beside the moment in Ghosh where you feel most implicated as a humanist.

Questions to carry into the reading

  • Ghosh argues literary imagination has failed to reckon with climate change. What does the failure reveal about what fiction, and scholarship, do and do not do?
  • Whom is your dissertation for? Would a non-specialist care about it?
  • Ghosh moves between criticism, political analysis, and memoir. What does the mixing of genres make possible, and what does it cost?
  • For Ghosh, the unthinkable is what imagination refuses to engage. What is unthinkable in your research area?

When we meet

A CRDM graduate joins us from 11:05 to 11:45 to talk about what the dissertation made possible: where the work went, whom it reached, and how humanistic research travels beyond the academy.

The “so what” gauntlet

Stakes often sit buried under disciplinary language. The gauntlet digs until it finds them.

  1. In groups of three, each of you presents your 150-word version.
  2. After each presentation, both listeners ask “so what?”, politely and without letting up, until the presenter reaches what matters to people outside the field.
  3. Write one sentence stating the real stakes.
  4. Read your sentence to the room. It belongs in your prospectus.

After we meet

  • Write three 100-word versions of your project for Week 13.
  • Choose a piece of scholarly communication you admire to bring next week.

Further reading, if this week opened something up

  • Ursula K. Le Guin, “The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction,” short and generative. In the Readings folder.
  • Anna Tsing, The Mushroom at the End of the World, humanistic inquiry attentive to entanglement, precarity, and possibility.
