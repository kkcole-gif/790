Movement I · Week 3 · Thursday, January 28

The Past Is Not Past

Benjamin insists the past is never settled. This week reads three of his essays against the objects you bring from your own research, and a current doctoral student joins us to talk about this stage of the program.

Read
Benjamin, Illuminations, three essays (about 74 pages)
Bring
One object from your research area, and one genuine question about the “Theses”
Guest
A current CRDM doctoral student
Due
No milestone this week

Before class

Read

  • Walter Benjamin, Illuminations, ed. Hannah Arendt, trans. Harry Zohn (Schocken, 2007). On Perusall:
  • “The Storyteller,” pages 83 to 109.
  • “The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction,” pages 217 to 251.
  • “Theses on the Philosophy of History,” pages 253 to 264. Short and dense. Give the “Theses” more time than their length suggests.

Write and bring

  • One object (a photograph, a text, an image, a sound clip) representing something unresolved in your research area. You need not explain it fully. Be ready to say why it matters.
  • A Perusall annotation marking one moment in the “Theses” you find cryptic, with a genuine question about it.

Questions to carry into the reading

  • Benjamin writes of history flashing up in a moment of danger. What does his claim do to your research questions?
  • What is the angel of history, and what storm drives it? How does the image land for you now?
  • Benjamin argues mechanical reproduction destroys aura. What has digital reproduction done to the texts and images you study?
  • Who is the storyteller, in Benjamin’s sense, in your own field?
  • What piece of history in your research area is not past?

In class

A current doctoral student joins us during the session to talk about this stage of the program and what it asks of students.

Session plan

Wreckage and promise gallery

Benjamin’s angel faces the wreckage of the past while a storm drives it forward. The gallery reads your objects the same way.

  1. Place your object, or a description of it, on the table. Five minutes of silent looking.
  2. In small groups, ask of each object: what wreckage does it hold, and what promise? How does Thesis IX apply?
  3. As a full group, name the patterns across the cohort’s objects.
  4. Write one sentence: “The piece of history I am working in the wreckage of is ___.” Keep it for your Scholarly Identity Statement.

After class

  • Read Mbembe for Week 4, and find an example of necropolitical logic to bring.
  • Keep your wreckage sentence for the Identity Statement due in Week 5.

Further reading

  • Hannah Arendt, “Walter Benjamin: 1892–1940,” her introduction to Illuminations, pages 1 to 55. Essential context.
  • Susan Buck-Morss, The Dialectics of Seeing, the most thorough account of Benjamin’s method.
