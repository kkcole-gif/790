The University Under Neoliberalism

Brown argues neoliberalism remakes the subject, the citizen, the student, and the scholar. This week reads her account of the university alongside your first milestone, a statement of who you are as a scholar.

Read
Brown, Undoing the Demos, Chapters 1 and 6 (about 87 pages)

Milestone 1, Scholarly Identity Statement, before class

Before class

Milestone due: Scholarly Identity Statement

About 500 words. Who are you as a scholar? What do you care about, what animates your research, and why does it matter? Where did your questions come from, and what do you want your work to do in the world? Write from conviction, for a reader who is not a hiring committee. A summary of your CV or a list of topics does not meet the threshold. 20% of the final grade. Submit through Canvas, by email, or in hard copy.

Full guide to the Scholarly Identity Statement milestone

Read

  • Wendy Brown, Undoing the Demos: Neoliberalism’s Stealth Revolution (Zone Books, 2015). Chapter 1, “Undoing Democracy,” pages 23 to 62, and Chapter 6, “Educating Human Capital,” pages 175 to 222. On Perusall, and available online through the library.
  • Optional: the Introduction, pages 7 to 21, for Brown’s core claim and method.

Write and bring

  • Your Scholarly Identity Statement, in a form you are willing to trade with a partner.
  • A Perusall annotation on one moment where Brown’s argument lands on you, beyond the abstraction.

Questions to carry into the reading

  • Brown argues neoliberalism remakes identity, values, and ways of relating to the world. Do you recognize the remaking in yourself?
  • What does it mean to pursue a PhD in the humanities under neoliberalism? What are you investing in, and what return do others expect?
  • Brown distinguishes homo politicus from homo oeconomicus. Which are you being trained to be? Which do you want to be?
  • What is the university for? Has Brown changed your answer?
  • If the PhD is part self-investment and part critical refusal, how do you hold both at once?

Identity statement exchange

A close reading of each other’s statements, looking for conviction.

  1. Exchange statements with a partner you have not yet worked with closely.
  2. Read for ten minutes. Underline the most alive sentence. Circle anything written for a committee instead of from conviction. Write one question the statement opens for you.
  3. Share responses, five minutes of conversation per statement.
  4. As a group, name what keeps appearing across the statements, what we seem least sure of, and what we seem most sure of.

After class

  • Read Federici for Week 6, and begin sketching your ideal committee.

Further reading

  • Martha Nussbaum, Not For Profit: Why Democracy Needs the Humanities, the affirmative case for humanistic education.
  • Kathleen Fitzpatrick, Generous Thinking, on the public role of the university.
  • David Harvey, A Brief History of Neoliberalism, a companion to Brown.
