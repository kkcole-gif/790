Movement III · Week 11 · Thursday, March 25

One-Dimensional Thinking and the Dissertation Imagination

Marcuse argues advanced industrial society absorbs and neutralizes dissent. Movement III opens by turning his critique on your first prospectus paragraph, then asks you to argue for your method on epistemological grounds.

Read
Marcuse, One-Dimensional Man, Introduction and Chapters 1 and 3 (about 53 pages)
Bring
A prospectus paragraph and its critique, plus your research question and three candidate methods
Due
No milestone this week

Before class

Read

  • Herbert Marcuse, One-Dimensional Man (Beacon Press, 1991). Introduction to the first edition, pages xi to xvii. Chapter 1, “The New Forms of Control,” pages 1 to 18. Chapter 3, “The Conquest of the Unhappy Consciousness,” pages 56 to 83. On Perusall.
  • Pages 4 to 8, on false and true needs, anchor the discussion.

Write and bring

  • Your first prospectus paragraph: the problem, why it matters, and who it is for.
  • A second paragraph offering the Marcusean critique of the first. Where does it reproduce the field’s assumptions? Where does it fail to imagine alternatives?
  • Your working research question, stated as a question, with three methods which might answer it and one sentence on what each would let you see.

Questions to carry into the reading

  • Marcuse argues we have lost the capacity to imagine alternatives. Do you agree? Where do you see the loss in your field?
  • What would a one-dimensional dissertation look like? Which assumptions would it reproduce without question?
  • Marcuse critiques academic language itself, the way technical discourse forecloses political thought. Do you recognize this in your field’s writing?

In class

The methods activity rests on three distinctions. A method is what you do: close reading, interviews, archival research, corpus analysis. A methodology is the framework which justifies the method, given what it assumes about knowing: rhetorical criticism, critical ethnography, feminist standpoint theory. An epistemology is the underlying claim about what counts as knowledge. “I will use discourse analysis” names a method. “Because language constitutes social reality, discourse analysis suits this question better than content analysis” makes a methodological argument.

Method, methodology, epistemology

Your committee will ask why your question requires your method. This activity has you make the argument first, against a partner who looks for its gaps.

  1. Five minutes: fill out a methodology card with your question, method, methodology, the argument linking them, one ruled-out alternative, and your greatest uncertainty.
  2. Ten minutes: trade cards. Your partner asks whether another method would answer the question as well, whether your epistemological claim does any work, and what the strongest counterargument is.
  3. Ten minutes: each of you names one mismatch, gap, or assumption the pressure test exposed.
  4. Fifteen minutes: draft a paragraph of three to five sentences stating your question, naming your method and its methodology, and arguing for your approach against a named alternative.
  5. Five minutes: two or three volunteers read their paragraphs. Is the epistemological argument visible?
